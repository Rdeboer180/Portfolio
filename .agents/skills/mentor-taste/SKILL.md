# Mentor Taste

Create LinkedIn writing that makes Ryan's work legible to exacting design leaders. Do not impersonate any mentor or manufacture their approval. Apply their evidenced standards while preserving Ryan's voice.

## Load the evidence

Read [references/mentor-lenses.md](references/mentor-lenses.md) before drafting. Use the individual lenses selectively; do not blend seven voices into a generic average.

## Establish the post's job

Infer one primary objective:

- demonstrate design judgment;
- show design-to-code or agentic capability;
- explain a consequential product decision;
- reveal a craft exploration;
- present shipped proof or a portfolio artifact;
- signal readiness for a specific role or team;
- contribute a useful industry point of view.

Ask at most one question only when the missing answer would materially change the claim, evidence, audience, or confidentiality boundary.

## Require an evidence packet

Extract what is actually known:

- artifact, prototype, shipped feature, or experiment;
- problem or tension;
- Ryan's specific decision and why it mattered;
- constraints, failed directions, or tradeoffs;
- AI/agent contribution versus Ryan's contribution;
- implementation reality: code, system, states, testing, or launch status;
- observable outcome, learning, or next question;
- visual evidence available for the post.

Never invent metrics, user feedback, team reactions, shipped status, tool usage, or causality. If proof is thin, write an exploration post rather than disguising it as impact.

## Choose one mentor-led angle

Choose the strongest lens for the evidence:

- **Krehel — encoded judgment:** explain the rule, detail, or reusable system that makes an interface better.
- **Geoco — consequential taste:** tell a human story about care, ambition, curation, or a risk worth taking.
- **Riddering — visible story:** make the work immediately understandable; show value and the person behind the pixels.
- **Tredly — undeniable hiring signal:** show end-to-end ownership, craft, software-building ability, speed, and initiative.
- **Choi — prototype as argument:** let a precise interaction, motion study, or coded experiment carry the thesis.
- **Crisan — taste as care:** show intentional iteration, human agency, system behavior, and the final 30% of refinement.
- **McNeill — foundations before automation:** show how tokens, components, standards, and team adoption give AI enough context to produce useful work.

Name the chosen lens in working notes, never in the post unless the user is explicitly discussing that person.

## Find an earned quote and tag opportunity

For every request, assess whether the work creates a substantive connection to one mentor's published thinking. Prefer the mentor whose idea the artifact demonstrates, complicates, or extends—not the most famous person or the person most likely to increase reach.

Add a quote and tag only when all are true:

- the connection is central to the post rather than decorative;
- the quote can be verified word-for-word in a public primary source;
- the source and original context support the meaning used in the post;
- Ryan's work adds evidence, experience, a respectful counterpoint, or a new application;
- tagging the mentor would give them a credible reason to care about the post.

Use at most one mentor quote and one mentor tag by default. Never tag all seven. Prefer a short quote fragment; paraphrase longer ideas and link to the source. Do not quote from search-result summaries, third-party paraphrases, or memory. If current verification is unavailable, recommend the connection without quotation marks and flag it for source verification.

Introduce the reference naturally:

- “Loredana Crisan describes taste as care. This project made that concrete for me…”
- “Jakub Krehel's work on encoding judgment into agent skills prompted a question…”
- “Jared Tredly keeps emphasizing working proof over positioning. Here is mine…”
- “Kirk McNeill's UI Collective work gave me a useful way to think about this: AI needs a design-system foundation before it can extend one responsibly.”

Do not write “inspired by” unless Ryan actually encountered the source before doing the work. Use “this connects with,” “this gave me language for,” or “I see a parallel with” when the relationship was recognized afterward.

Tag using the mentor's full LinkedIn name only after confirming the intended profile from [references/mentor-lenses.md](references/mentor-lenses.md). Treat the rendered `@Name` as a suggested tag for Ryan to select in LinkedIn's composer; plain text alone may not create a live mention.

Skip the quote or tag when it would feel promotional, opportunistic, repetitive, weakly related, or likely to burden the person with no meaningful contribution. Never imply that a mentor reviewed, endorsed, mentored, or collaborated on Ryan's work unless that happened.

## Build the post

Use this default architecture flexibly:

1. **Specific tension:** one concrete observation, decision, surprise, or disagreement. Avoid generic hooks.
2. **Artifact early:** identify what Ryan made by the first short paragraph.
3. **Judgment:** explain the non-obvious decision or principle.
4. **Proof:** give two or three concrete details from the work.
5. **Human/AI boundary:** when relevant, state what agents accelerated and what Ryan selected, rejected, shaped, tested, or implemented.
6. **Consequence:** state what changed, what was learned, or what the work now enables.
7. **Open edge:** end with a real question, invitation, or next experiment only when it advances the discussion.

Lead with the work, not career aspiration. Let employability be inferred from evidence.

## Voice rules

- Sound like a thoughtful builder reflecting on real work.
- Prefer concrete nouns and active verbs.
- Use short paragraphs for scanning, but preserve natural sentence rhythm.
- Keep useful technical specificity; translate only what blocks comprehension.
- Make one defensible claim rather than several inflated claims.
- Admit uncertainty when it is part of the work.
- Credit collaborators and source ideas precisely.
- Use Ryan's established vocabulary when present: systems, craft, implementation, evidence, constraints, iteration, ownership.

Avoid:

- “thrilled/excited/humbled to share” launch boilerplate;
- “AI-native,” “game-changing,” “revolutionary,” or “10x” without direct proof;
- fake vulnerability or manufactured controversy;
- numbered lessons added only for engagement;
- process inventories that hide the decision;
- calling prompting alone design engineering;
- claiming authorship over agent or collaborator work;
- engagement bait, empty questions, or hashtag clouds;
- writing as if all seven mentors share one aesthetic.

## Apply the recognition test

Before delivering, score each dimension from 0–2:

- **Point of view:** Is there an opinionated, defensible design judgment?
- **Artifact:** Can the reader identify the actual thing made?
- **Specificity:** Are important details concrete enough to verify?
- **Craft:** Does the writing reveal care beyond first-pass generation?
- **Systems:** Does it connect the artifact to behavior, constraints, or reuse?
- **Building:** Does it show movement from idea toward working reality?
- **Authorship:** Is Ryan's contribution distinct from tools and collaborators?
- **Human value:** Is the user, team, or creative purpose visible?
- **Restraint:** Is hype lower than evidence?
- **Memorability:** Is there one line or idea worth carrying forward?

Revise any dimension scoring 0. Do not force every post to maximize every dimension.

## Deliver

Return:

1. one polished post;
2. a concise **Mentor signal** note naming the two or three standards the draft demonstrates;
3. a concise **Evidence gap** only when missing proof weakens the post;
4. a **Quote + tag opportunity** containing the verified quote, primary-source link, suggested `@Full Name`, and one sentence explaining why the connection is earned—or “No earned mentor tag for this post”;
5. an optional visual recommendation when a specific artifact would materially strengthen credibility.

Keep the post ready to paste. Do not include citations inside ordinary LinkedIn copy unless the post directly discusses sourced ideas. When external claims are included, provide source links outside the post.
